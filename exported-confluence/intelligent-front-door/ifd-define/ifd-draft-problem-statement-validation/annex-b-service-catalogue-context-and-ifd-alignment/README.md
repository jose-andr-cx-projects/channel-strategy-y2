# Annex B - Service Catalogue Context and IFD Alignment

## 1.1. Purpose

This annex summarises the current City of Melbourne Service Catalogue and explains how it relates to the Intelligent Front Door Define work.

The Service Catalogue provides the organisational structure for understanding:

- what services City of Melbourne delivers;
- how services are grouped;
- who owns and delivers them;
- the value they are intended to create; and
- how they contribute to broader organisational outcomes.

For the Intelligent Front Door, the catalogue is an important **organisational reference model**.

It should help us connect customer intent to existing services without requiring customers themselves to understand or navigate City of Melbourne's internal service structure.

---

## 1.2. B1. Service Data Dashboard

The **Service Data Dashboard (V1.0)** is described as the digital home of City of Melbourne's Service Catalogue.

It provides a single place to explore:

- the services City of Melbourne delivers;
- who delivers them; and
- how those services create value for customers, community and city.

V1.0 is explicitly an initial iteration.

The stated intention is for more service data to be added over time, creating a richer understanding of services and strengthening how they are planned, managed and continuously improved across the organisation.

The dashboard currently provides three connected views:

**Service Catalogue**

**Service Profile**

**Service Glossary**

Together they provide:

> **structure → service context → shared terminology**

The catalogue view also supports filtering by organisational and strategic attributes such as division/branch, Service Area, delivery team and Council Plan alignment.

---

# 2. B2. The catalogue is organised around services, not channels

The catalogue presents services through a service-based structure rather than through individual channels, webpages, forms or systems.

At the highest level, services are grouped into **Service Areas**.

The dashboard distinguishes between:

### 2.1.1. Public-facing services

Services delivered directly to residents, visitors, businesses or the community.

### 2.1.2. Public-enabling services

Services that enable, support or improve the delivery of public-facing services.

### 2.1.3. Enterprise services

Internal services that support the organisation to operate and deliver its public-facing services.

The glossary gives examples such as:

- **Public-facing:** Parking, Maternal Child Health, Waste and Recycling;
- **Public-enabling:** Aboriginal Empowerment, Community Engagement;
- **Enterprise:** People and Culture, Data and Insights, Technology.

### 2.1.4. IFD implication

The primary Intelligent Front Door problem is concerned with customers reaching **public-facing services**.

Public-enabling and enterprise services may support that experience, but they would not normally be presented to customers as destinations.

This distinction can help prevent the Intelligent Front Door becoming a mirror of the internal organisational catalogue.

# 3. B3. Service taxonomy

The Service Glossary establishes several important concepts.

## 3.1. Service Area

A **Service Area** is a themed grouping of related services with a common:

- purpose;
- customer cohort; or
- strategic intent.

It provides the broadest organisational grouping within the catalogue.

## 3.2. Service

A **Service** is described as a structured combination of activities, resources and interactions that the organisation provides to help people:

- meet a need;
- solve a problem; or
- make life easier.

Services are the means through which value is created for:

- customers;
- community; and
- the city.

Importantly for the Intelligent Front Door, the glossary recognises that:

> a service may involve multiple teams, touchpoints and delivery partners while being experienced by the user as one continuous journey towards an outcome.

This is highly aligned with the IFD proposition.

The customer does not necessarily experience organisational boundaries, systems or delivery teams as separate services.

They experience an attempt to achieve an outcome.

## 3.3. Service Offering

The written glossary describes a **Service Offering** as the ongoing, repeatable activities, programs and processes delivered by a service as part of day-to-day operations to create value for customers.

### 3.3.1. Current working hierarchy for IFD

Based on the written definitions and the Service Profile structure, the working hierarchy remains:

> **Service Area → Service → Service Offering**

The Intelligent Front Door then introduces customer-facing decision logic beneath or across this organisational structure:

> **Service Area → Service → Service Offering → Customer Intent / Contact Reason → Interaction Mode → Information Need → Support Need → Authentication Need → Existing Pathway**

This remains a **working IFD model**, not an amendment to the Service Catalogue taxonomy.

# 4. B4. Taxonomy point to validate

There is a terminology inconsistency in the current dashboard material that should remain visible.

The **Service Taxonomy diagram** in the Service Glossary displays:

> **Service Area → Service → Scope**

with examples of Scope.

However, the written definitions alongside the diagram define:

> **Service Area → Service → Service Offering**

and the Service Profile also contains a **Service Offering** section.

### 4.1.1. Validation question

> **Is "Scope" an intended taxonomy level, an earlier label, or another concept that sits underneath Service?**

Until confirmed, the IFD work should avoid creating its own resolution.

For current Define work we can continue using **Service Offering** where that terminology is supported by the profile and written glossary, while marking the hierarchy as subject to Service Catalogue owner validation.

# 5. B5. What a Service Profile contains

The Service Profile adds richer organisational context around a Service Area.

The example supplied for **Communications and Marketing** shows that a profile can contain:

- Service Area;
- Division;
- Branch;
- Service Owner;
- Delivery Teams;
- description and purpose;
- Council Plan alignment;
- Key Performance Indicators; and
- individual Service Offerings and descriptions.

The profile therefore extends the catalogue beyond a classification structure.

It begins to connect:

> **service → ownership → delivery → strategic contribution → performance → offerings**

The current example also notes that consistent performance measures are still to be developed with service teams, reinforcing that the dashboard is an evolving service-management asset rather than a finished dataset.

# 6. B6. Parking in the current Service Catalogue

The Service Catalogue currently places **Parking** within:

> **Service Area: Parking, Transport and Mobility**

Within that Service Area, the catalogue displays at least two Services:

- **Roads and Public Infrastructure**
- **Parking**

This gives the Intelligent Front Door a confirmed organisational anchor for the current Parking Define work:

> **Parking, Transport and Mobility → Parking**

The supplied catalogue view does **not** show the detailed Parking Service Offerings.

Those mappings therefore still require validation against the authoritative Service Catalogue data or relevant service owners.

We should not infer the formal Service Offering structure from CRM case names, webpages or the IFD use-case grid alone.

# 7. B7. How the Service Catalogue and Intelligent Front Door fit together

The two models solve different problems.

| Service Catalogue | Intelligent Front Door |
| --- | --- |
| Describes how City of Melbourne understands and organises its services | Helps customers recognise what they are trying to achieve |
| Provides organisational service taxonomy | Provides customer-intent and routing logic |
| Defines Service Areas, Services and Service Offerings | Connects intents to appropriate existing services and pathways |
| Supports ownership and service management | Supports findability, understanding and navigation |
| Can connect services to delivery teams and organisational outcomes | Can determine information, support and authentication needs |
| Primarily an organisational service model | Primarily a customer decision and navigation layer |

The IFD should therefore **use the Service Catalogue rather than replicate it**.

# 8. B8. Emerging relationship

A useful way to describe the relationship is:

**Customer language**

> "I got a fine and I think it is wrong."

↓

**Customer intent**

> Request a review of a fine

↓

**IFD decision / routing layer**

> What action is the customer actually trying to take?  
> What do they need to understand?  
> What support is needed?  
> Is authentication or authority required?

↓

**Service Catalogue alignment**

> Parking, Transport and Mobility  
> → Parking  
> → relevant Service Offering

↓

**Existing service pathway**

> existing review process / digital pathway / assisted pathway

The catalogue provides the **organisational destination**.

The Intelligent Front Door provides the **customer-facing logic for reaching it**.

---

# 9. B9. Why customer intent should remain separate from the catalogue taxonomy

The Service Catalogue and IFD should not be collapsed into a single taxonomy.

A Service Offering describes what City of Melbourne delivers.

A customer intent describes what the customer is trying to achieve.

Those are related, but they are not always one-to-one.

For example:

> **"I need a parking permit"**

may ultimately connect with several different permit-related offerings or pathways.

Similarly:

> **"Can I park here?"**

may initially require authoritative information rather than entry into a transaction.

And:

> **"Someone else was driving"**

expresses a customer intent that must be translated into the appropriate infringement-related service pathway.

This translation layer is potentially one of the most important functions of the Intelligent Front Door.

---

# 10. B10. Potential strategic value

The connection between the Service Catalogue and Intelligent Front Door creates a potentially reusable organisational capability.

Instead of independently organising:

- webpages;
- forms;
- contact-centre knowledge;
- digital assistants;
- search;
- service navigation; and
- future AI interfaces

around separate structures, customer intent could potentially be mapped back to a common service model.

Conceptually:

> **customer language → intent → service → authoritative information → pathway**

This could allow the Service Catalogue to become an important reference layer for customer-experience architecture without turning the catalogue itself into the customer interface.

This is an **emerging opportunity to test**, not a confirmed requirement of the Service Catalogue or Intelligent Front Door.

# 11. B11. Questions to validate with stakeholders

## 11.1. Catalogue structure

- Is **Service Area → Service → Service Offering** the correct hierarchy to use?
- What does **Scope** represent in the glossary taxonomy diagram?
- Which source should be treated as authoritative if those terms differ?

## 11.2. Parking alignment

- Is **Parking, Transport and Mobility → Parking** the correct anchor for all six current IFD use cases?
- What are the authoritative Parking Service Offerings beneath it?
- Do any current use cases cross multiple Services or Service Areas?

## 11.3. Customer intent

- At what level should customer intent connect to the catalogue?
- Can one intent connect to more than one Service Offering?
- Can multiple intents legitimately connect to the same offering?

## 11.4. Information

- Could Service Catalogue metadata help identify the authoritative information associated with each service?
- Where should customer-facing service knowledge be governed?
- What information belongs to the Service Catalogue versus other knowledge sources?

## 11.5. Ownership

- Can the catalogue help the IFD identify the accountable service owner when service information, routing or pathways need correction?
- How should changes to service structure flow through to customer navigation?

## 11.6. Future reuse

- Could the same intent-to-service mapping support web navigation, search, assisted service and future AI-enabled interactions?
- What minimum service metadata would be required to make that possible?

# 12. B12. Define position

For the current Define phase, use the following working principle:

> **The Service Catalogue describes City of Melbourne's service model. The Intelligent Front Door should translate customer intent into that model and route customers towards the appropriate existing service pathway.**

The objective is not to expose the organisation's taxonomy to customers.

The objective is to make that taxonomy **useful behind the experience**.
